## UK jobless rate rises to 5.2% as pay growth cools

**Falling payrolls and easing wage gains strengthen the case for a December cut in borrowing costs, though one leading bank economist expects policymakers to wait.**

UK unemployment rose to 5.2% in the three months to September, up from 5.0% in the previous three months, the Office for National Statistics said. That already exceeds the 5.1% that independent forecasters, on average, expect for the final quarter of the year, according to HM Treasury's November comparison.

The pay figures matter more for interest rates. Annual growth in regular earnings, excluding bonuses, slowed to 3.6% from 3.9%. The early estimate of payrolled employees showed a fall of 42,000 in October, and vacancies dropped by 18,000 on the quarter to 701,000.

The data go to the heart of the argument that divided the Bank of England this month, when it held Bank Rate at 3.75% by six votes to three, with the minority backing a cut to 3.5%. Its next decision is on 17 December.

Early reaction from economists largely read the figures as tipping the balance towards a December cut. Kieran Dobbs, chief UK economist at Pantheon Macroeconomics, told Reuters it was "the clearest signal yet that the labour market has turned" and that he now expects a cut to 3.5% next month. Capital Economics said the labour market was loosening faster than the Bank had projected in November, while Danni Hewson, head of financial analysis at AJ Bell, said the door to a December cut "looks to have swung open".

Marcus Vey, chief UK economist at Deutsche Bank, disagrees. He told the Guardian he expects the Bank to hold in December and wait until February, arguing that "regular pay at 3.6% is still running well above the roughly 3% consistent with the inflation target" once weak productivity is taken into account.

Two-year gilt yields, the most sensitive to rate expectations, were 6 basis points lower at 3.62% by mid-morning, a bigger fall than at longer maturities, and sterling was down 0.35% against the dollar.

Wednesday's October inflation figures are the next test: a soft reading would strengthen the majority case, a firm one Vey's.
