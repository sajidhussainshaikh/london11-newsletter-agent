## Jobless rate climbs to 5.2% as pay growth cools

**Weaker hiring strengthens the case for a Bank of England cut in December, though one leading economist expects policymakers will hold until February.**

Britain's unemployment rate rose to 5.2% in the three months to September, from 5.0% in the previous quarter, the Office for National Statistics said. That already exceeds the 5.1% average that independent forecasters, surveyed by HM Treasury this month, expected for the final quarter of the year.

The detail was weaker still. The early estimate of payrolled employees fell by 42,000 in October, and vacancies dropped by 18,000 on the quarter to 701,000. Annual growth in regular pay, excluding bonuses, slowed to 3.6% from 3.9%.

That combination sharpens the rates debate at the Bank of England, which held Bank Rate at 3.75% on 5 November when three of nine policymakers backed a cut to 3.5%. Its next decision is on 17 December.

Most early reaction read the figures as tipping the balance towards a cut. Pantheon Macroeconomics chief UK economist Kieran Dobbs told Reuters he now expects a reduction to 3.5% next month, saying slower pay growth "removes the main obstacle the hawks had pointed to". Capital Economics UK economist Ruth Calder said the labour market was loosening faster than the Bank of England had projected in its November Monetary Policy Report. AJ Bell head of financial analysis Danni Hewson said the door to a December cut "looks to have swung open".

Deutsche Bank chief UK economist Marcus Vey is not persuaded. He told the Guardian he expects a hold in December and a move in February, arguing that regular pay growth of 3.6% remains well above a rate consistent with the inflation target. "One soft quarter of labour data is not enough for a majority to move before Christmas," he said.

Gilts rallied across the curve. By mid-morning the two-year yield, the most sensitive to rate expectations, was 6bp lower at 3.62%, while sterling was 0.35% weaker at $1.3120.

Wednesday's October inflation figures are the next test: a soft reading would strengthen the case for December, while firm price pressures would support Vey's case for patience.
